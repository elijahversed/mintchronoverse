_Amazing Fantasy_ was already on its way out. Marvel had decided to cancel the low-selling anthology series, and its final issue became, almost by accident, one of the most consequential single issues in comics history.

## What happens

Peter Parker is introduced as exactly the kind of character superhero comics generally avoided putting front and center: a bullied, socially isolated high schooler with no particular confidence. At a science demonstration, he's bitten by a spider that had been exposed to radiation, and gains proportional spider-strength, wall-crawling, and a precognitive early version of what would later be called his "spider-sense."

His first instinct isn't heroism — it's using his new abilities for money and attention. That choice, and the consequence that follows it — the death of his Uncle Ben at the hands of a robber Peter had chosen not to stop earlier — is where the character's defining ethic comes from.

<Note>
  The line most associated with this issue — the idea that power carries responsibility with it — isn't dialogue you'll find reproduced here, but it's the thesis the rest of the character's history has been built on for over sixty years.
</Note>

## Why it matters

Prior superhero leads tended to be confident, capable, and largely unbothered by ordinary problems. Peter Parker was the opposite on all three counts, and that gap is why the issue resonated: readers weren't watching a hero solve problems, they were watching a teenager fail, lose someone because of that failure, and then commit to doing better.

The issue sold well enough that Spider-Man was given his own ongoing series the following year, launching a publishing history that's continued, in one form or another, ever since.
